Why Belonging Matters — The Research Case

🧠

Research consistently shows that students who feel a sense of belonging perform better academically, persist longer, and report higher wellbeing — and that this is especially true for international students. (Rees, 2024)

What Good Communication Looks Like

🔊

Clear

Simple sentences. No jargon or contractions. A measured pace. Say "do not" not "don't."

😊

Friendly

Smile. Use their name. Acknowledge their effort to communicate in a foreign language.

🤝

Supportive

Offer options, not dead ends. Normalise asking again. End every interaction positively.

Part 1

Understanding the Cultural Context

Before specific phrases, it helps to understand why communication with international students can be challenging — and what creates misunderstandings.

Different Communication Cultures

🇯🇵

Japanese Style

Often indirect. Silence is comfortable. "Reading the room" is valued. Disagreement is rarely expressed openly.

🌍

International Expectations

Many expect direct answers. Silence may feel like rejection. They may ask the same question repeatedly or push back directly.

⚡

Where Friction Happens

The gap between these styles is where misunderstandings occur — not from bad intentions, but different assumptions.

Common Cultural Friction Points

!
The polite "Yes" problem→ Many students say "yes" to avoid embarrassment. Always verify with a specific check question.
!
Silence being misread→ Staff silence may feel like coldness or disapproval to international students.
!
Indirect refusals causing confusion→ "That may be difficult..." means "no" in Japan but "maybe" to many international students.
!
Emotional expression differences→ A student who seems urgent or expressive is not being rude — this may be their normal style.

Different is not wrong — it's just different

International students are navigating an unfamiliar system in a foreign language, often under stress. Every clear, kind interaction supports Science Tokyo's mission.

Gestures Aren't Universal Either

👌

The "OK" Sign

Vulgar or offensive in Brazil, and read negatively in parts of the Middle East and Germany.

👍

Thumbs Up

Considered offensive in parts of the Middle East, West Africa, and some Latin American countries.

🤷

Head Wobble / Tilt

In India, a side-to-side wobble often signals agreement — not "no" or uncertainty.

When in doubt, ask

Check what a gesture means before using it with someone new, rather than assuming it translates.

Part 2

Common Communication Gaps

Small language habits create big misunderstandings. Here are the five most common gaps — and how to close them.

The Top 5 Gaps

01
Assuming silence = understanding→ Always check: "Does that make sense?"
02
Using passive voice or bureaucratic phrases→ "You need to..." becomes "Could you please..."
03
Speaking too fast or using contractions→ Slow down. Say "do not" not "don't"
04
Saying "No" directly without explanation→ Offer a reason + an alternative path
05
Repeating the same words when not understood→ Rephrase — don't just repeat louder

Before & After

Before

"The deadline is the 30th. You have to submit it through the portal."

After

"The deadline is June 30th. Could you please submit the form online? I can show you how."

Before

"That's not possible."

After

"I'm afraid that isn't available right now, but let me check what options we have for you."

"Yes" Doesn't Always Mean Yes

"You haven't submitted the form yet, have you?"

Negative questions flip the logic of yes/no answers between languages.

Not Submitted

English: "No, I haven't."

Japanese: "Hai" (Yes) — correct, not submitted

Already Submitted

English: "Yes, I have."

Japanese: "Iie" (No) — incorrect, I have submitted

Avoid the ambiguity

Confirm with a direct statement — "OK, so it's not submitted yet?"

Part 3

Counter & Procedure Language

At the service counter, clear step-by-step language reduces confusion and builds trust.

Essential Phrases

Opening
  • "How can I help you today?"
  • "Could I have your student ID?"
  • "What is this regarding?"
Explaining Steps
  • "First, you will need to..."
  • "After that, please..."
  • "The final step is to..."
Checking In
  • "Does that make sense?"
  • "Would you like me to write that?"
  • "Shall I show you on the map?"

Sample Dialogue: Certificate Request

Student
Excuse me. I need... enrollment certificate. For visa.
Staff
Of course! Could I have your student ID card, please?
Student
(hands over card)
Staff
Thank you. There are two steps. First, fill in this form. Second, come back in three business days. Does that make sense?
Student
Three days? OK...
Staff
Yes — three working days. Would you like me to write the collection date on this slip for you?

Sample Dialogue: Lost on Campus

Student
Sorry... International Office? Where?
Staff
Of course! It's in Building 3. Go straight — then turn left. Second floor. Room 201.
Student
Building 3... second floor?
Staff
Yes, exactly. Shall I draw a quick map for you?
Student
Yes please! Thank you.

Part 4

Rules, Requests & Saying No Softly

Being firm and kind at the same time is a skill. These tools help you set limits without closing doors.

The Softening Toolkit

Modal verbs
"You must" → "You will need to" / "Could you please..."
I'm afraid...
"I'm afraid that isn't possible at the moment."
Unfortunately
"Unfortunately we cannot do that today, but..."
Let me check
"Let me check what I can do for you."
No + Alternative
"That form is only online. I can send you the link right now."

The CARE Formula

Context → Acknowledgment → Reason → Exit (alternative)

A "no" with a path forward is always better than a dead end.

CARE Formula in Action

📋 Student asks for a same-day certificate
"I understand you need it quickly. Unfortunately, certificates take three business days. Would it help if I gave you a letter confirming your enrolment? That is available today."
📚 Student's progress is below the required standard
"I would like to be honest with you about your progress — right now, you're not quite at the required level. Let's look at what's realistic between now and the deadline, and build a plan together. Would that help?"
📅 Student asks to change course after deadline
"I completely understand. Unfortunately, the deadline was last Friday. I would suggest emailing your supervisor — they may be able to help. Would you like their contact details?"

Part 5

Supporting Students Who Struggle to Express Themselves

Sometimes a student knows what they need but cannot find the words. Creating a patient, safe space is itself a communication skill.

Reading the Signs

Signal
Your Response
Long pause before answering
"Take your time — there's no rush."
Repeating your question back
"Yes, exactly. Do you have it with you?"
"Yes" to everything (politeness)
Ask a yes/no check: "So, is the form ready?"
Showing phone / translation app
"Of course — please go ahead and translate."
Looking confused or anxious
"It's okay, take your time." Slow down. Offer paper.

Rephrasing Without Repeating

Strategy: Use shorter sentences
Instead of: "You will need to submit the required documentation to the administrative office by the end of this month."

Try: "Please bring this form here. By March 31st."
Strategy: Use numbers and gestures
Instead of: "Come back in a few days."

Try: "Come back on Thursday. That is three days. (point to calendar)"
Strategy: Write it down
Instead of: Repeating the same sentence.

Try: "Let me write this for you." (writes key info on a slip)

Starting Real Conversations

Beyond "How Are You?"

"How's everything going so far?"

"Is there anything I can help with — even something small?"

"How are you finding things here so far?"

Encouraging Quiet Students

Ask open questions, not yes/no ones.

Pause after asking — silence while thinking is OK.

"What do you think?" — then wait.

Feeling unwell?

"Would you like the address for the Health Center or a nearby clinic?"

Part 6

Written Communication with International Students

Email and written notices are often the first point of contact. The same principles apply — and written mistakes are harder to take back.

Key Principles for Written English

✂️

Keep it Short

One idea per sentence. Two to three sentences per paragraph. Avoid long formal blocks of text.

📋

Use Structure

Number steps. Use bullet points for lists. Put the most important information first — not last.

🤝

Stay Warm

Open with something supportive. Close with an offer to help. Formal emails can still have a human tone.

Email: Before & After

Before

"Regarding your inquiry, please be advised that the aforementioned documentation is required to be submitted prior to the commencement of the processing period."

After

"Thank you for your message. Please submit your documents before June 30th. If you have any questions, please feel free to contact us."

Opening
"Thank you for your message." / "I hope this email finds you well."
Saying No
"I'm afraid we are unable to..." / "Unfortunately..., however..."
Closing
"Please do not hesitate to contact us." / "Best regards,"

Key Takeaways

1
You do not need perfect English — you need clear, kind English.
2
Slow down, check for understanding, and never just repeat louder.
3
Soft language — I'm afraid / Unfortunately / Let me check — protects relationships.
4
Always offer an alternative. A "no" with a path forward beats a dead end.
5
Every interaction is a chance to represent Science Tokyo's international spirit.
